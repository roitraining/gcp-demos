# 1.2 · Reading a histogram

*Part 1 · What a metric is*

> [!NOTE]
> **Why you are here.** `count` and `sum` collapse every value into two numbers.
> This page draws the two fields underneath them, `bucket_counts` and
> `explicit_bounds`, so you see the shape they hide and why a metric keeps it.

In 1.1 every datapoint carried two more fields. `explicit_bounds` is a fixed
ladder of edges; `bucket_counts` says how many recorded values fell between each
pair of edges. Together they are the histogram. One turn cannot show a shape:
two values land in one bucket and look just like a single number. So this page
asks ten turns and draws the result.

**👉 Run ten turns and draw the histogram.**

[examples/03_histogram_shape.py](../../examples/03_histogram_shape.py) asks the
weather question ten times against an in-memory reader, then draws the
`gen_ai.client.token.usage` (output) datapoint straight from its own
`bucket_counts` and `explicit_bounds`.

**Command:**

```bash
.venv/bin/python examples/03_histogram_shape.py
```

**Expected output:** a summary line, then one bar per non-empty bucket. Token
counts vary run to run, so your bars will differ a little.

```console
gen_ai.client.token.usage  (output tokens)
count=20  sum=1323  min=26  max=95

 25–50 | █████████████ 3
 50–75 | ████████████████████████████████████████ 9
75–100 | ████████████████████████████████████ 8
```

Each bar is one entry of `bucket_counts`, labeled with the `explicit_bounds`
edges around it. The `3` is three model calls that emitted between 25 and 50
output tokens; the `9` is nine calls between 50 and 75. The 20 model calls (ten
turns, two calls each) are the same 20 that `count` reports, now placed on a
range instead of summed into one number.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **What it means: the buckets are the shape `count` and `sum` throw away.**
> `count=20` and `sum=1323` give an average near 66 output tokens per call. The
> buckets add that most calls sit in the 50-to-75 band with a tail reaching 100,
> and `min`/`max` pin the run between 26 and 95. Same recording, but now you can
> see the spread, not just its midpoint.

## What a histogram is for

An average lies when values spread out. Ten fast turns and one slow turn average
to something that looks fine, and the slow turn, the one a user actually felt,
vanishes into the mean. A histogram keeps the values apart. On the chart above,
a slow turn would raise a bar in a high bucket you can point at and count, no
matter how many fast turns sit in the low ones.

That is why ADK records durations and token counts as histograms rather than
running totals. The buckets are pre-aggregated in the process, so keeping the
whole distribution costs the same whether you serve ten turns or ten million:
the bars grow taller, the number of bars does not.

> [!NOTE]
> The bars only answer "how many, and roughly how big." Turning the same buckets
> into a single "95% of turns finish under N" number is a percentile, which needs
> real volume behind it. Part 3 puts a load through these histograms and reads
> that number off the buckets.
